Geometric Unity asked you to picture a 14-dimensional space and a map, π, that sends each of its points down to our 4D spacetime. That is a huge leap to imagine all at once.

Here you take one step at a time. You add one extra direction of space and watch a simpler kind of projection, a perspective shadow, bring it down to your screen.

Two differences matter. Spacetime's 4 dimensions are 3 of space plus 1 of time, but the tesseract's 4th dimension, w, is another direction of space. And Geometric Unity is an unproven proposal, while the tesseract is settled mathematics.

A tesseract is a 4D cube. It needs a 4th direction of space, called w, at right angles to x, y, and z. We cannot point in that direction, but mathematics handles it easily.

Just as a cube's surface is 6 squares folded up in 3D, a tesseract's boundary is 8 cubes folded up in 4D. What you see is its shadow: squashed from 4D into 3D, then from 3D onto your flat screen.

Slide a point and it traces a line. Slide the line sideways and it sweeps out a square. Slide the square up and you get a cube. Slide the cube in the w direction and you get a tesseract.

Every step, you keep the old shape, add a copy, and join each old corner to its copy. So corners double: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16. Edges become old edges × 2 plus one new edge per old corner: 0, 1, 4, 12, 32.

Try the 0D to 4D buttons to watch each step happen.

A cube's surface is made of 6 squares. A tesseract's boundary is made of 8 cubes, called cells: one pair for each of the 4 directions x, y, z, and w.

When the shadow is seen straight on, it looks like a small cube inside a big cube, joined by 6 frustums (pyramids with their tips cut off). That stretching is caused by perspective. In 4D, all 8 cells are identical, perfect cubes.

Each of the 24 squares lies flat in one of 6 coordinate planes, the pair of directions it stretches along: red XY, orange XZ, gold XW, green YZ, cyan YW, violet ZW. That gives 4 squares of each color.

Squares of the same color are parallel in 4D, like opposite faces of a cube. In the shadow they get stretched by different amounts, but in the real shape they stay parallel. Use the Highlight buttons to pick out one set.

Every rotation happens in a plane. In 3D only one direction is left over, so we call that line the "axis." In 4D, a rotation in the XW plane leaves a whole plane (YZ) unchanged, so there is no single axis.

3D has 3 planes to turn in. 4D has 6: XY, XZ, XW, YZ, YW, ZW. Here the shape turns in XW and YW. In the shadow, cubes seem to pass through each other and turn inside out, but in 4D nothing passes through anything. The rigid shape just turns, and its shadow changes.

We use perspective twice. First, points farther away in w are drawn smaller, which turns 4D into 3D. Then points farther away in z are drawn smaller, which turns 3D into 2D.

A cube seen face-on in perspective looks like a small square inside a big square. In the same way, a tesseract looks like a small cube inside a big cube. The distortion is not a mistake. It is depth in a direction you cannot see directly.

Arrow keys rotate the shape. Space bar starts or stops auto-rotation.
