Engage · Phenomenon

The Puddle That Disappeared

After a rainstorm, puddles sit on the sidewalk. A few hours later they are gone, with no drain in sight. The water did not disappear. It moved, and it is still moving.

💧
Real World Phenomenon

Water Within a Closed System

Earth is a closed system for water. Water does not leave the system or get added to it as part of the water cycle. Instead, it moves among reservoirs (places that store water) within Earth and changes location and state. A closed system does not mean one closed-loop pathway. How could the puddle's water move to another reservoir?

Water can follow many different pathways as it moves through Earth's closed water system.
🤔
Make a prediction: What helps water change state and move among reservoirs within the closed Earth system?
Here's the big idea

The best answer is B. Solar energy supplies much of the energy for evaporation: liquid water changes to invisible vapor. Gravity pulls precipitation downward and helps water flow downhill. These are different roles: the Sun is an energy source, and gravity is a force. Water follows many pathways and can remain stored along the way.

Where we're headed: First we'll compare solar energy and gravity. Then we'll explore processes that connect reservoirs and build an example journey with an alternative route.

Explore · The Two Drivers

What Moves the Water

Solar energy and gravity play different roles in the water cycle. Energy can help water change state; gravity is a force that affects its motion.

🔄
A Connected System

Earth is a closed system for water. Water moves among reservoirs and can stay stored in oceans, lakes, rivers, soil, groundwater, snow and ice, the atmosphere, and living things. A lake can lose liquid water by evaporation or gain it from rain and streams.

We call this connected movement and storage the water cycle. Water need not visit every reservoir or return to the ocean before moving again. Global recycling does not mean usable water is equally available everywhere at all times.

☀️ Powered by the Sun
  • The Sun supplies energy for much evaporation
  • Supplies energy for evaporation from oceans, lakes, rivers, and wet soil
  • Supports evaporation inside leaves during transpiration
⚖️ Pulled by Gravity
  • Gravity pulls water down toward the ground
  • Drives precipitation falling from clouds
  • Helps runoff flow downhill and water move through the ground
💡
Energy and force: Solar energy helps liquid water become vapor. Gravity pulls water downward and acts throughout the Earth system; it does not switch on after clouds form.
📚 Instructional Design
Why this section exists
  • Distinguish solar energy (energy source) from gravity (force).
  • Gravity acts throughout; it does not switch on after condensation.

Explore · Liquid to Vapor

Water Becomes Invisible Vapor

Water can enter the atmosphere from exposed wet surfaces and from plants. In both cases, liquid water changes into invisible water vapor.

☀️
From Liquid to Vapor

Liquid water can change into water vapor, an invisible gas. Evaporation happens at exposed water surfaces, including oceans, rivers, lakes, and wet soil. Solar energy supplies much of the energy for evaporation in Earth's water cycle.

Key idea: Evaporation

Evaporation is liquid water changing into invisible water vapor. It does not require boiling or direct sunlight. Movement upward in the air is not the state change itself. Most evaporation in the global water cycle occurs over the oceans, which cover most of the planet.

Key idea: Transpiration

Plants move water too. They absorb water through their roots, and water moves through the plant. During transpiration, some water evaporates inside leaves and exits as vapor through tiny openings in the leaves.

🍃
Connected reservoirs: Evaporation transfers water from exposed wet surfaces to the air. Transpiration connects soil water, plants, and the atmosphere. Solar energy supports these liquid-to-vapor changes.
📚 Instructional Design
Why this section exists

  • Trace roots to leaves to vapor; evaporation does not require boiling or direct sunlight.

Explore · Water in the Sky

Clouds and Rain

When moist air cools enough, some invisible water vapor can become tiny liquid droplets. Clouds contain droplets and/or ice crystals, not visible water vapor.

☁️
Cooling Makes Clouds

Moist air can cool enough for some water vapor to condense into tiny liquid droplets. These droplets can form clouds. Clouds may also contain ice crystals, or both droplets and ice crystals.

Key idea: Condensation

Condensation is water vapor changing into liquid droplets when moist air cools enough. It is the opposite of evaporation: vapor becomes liquid instead of liquid becoming vapor. Cloud formation and falling precipitation are different processes.

Key idea: Precipitation

Cloud droplets and/or ice particles can grow large enough to fall as precipitation. Gravity pulls them downward. Precipitation reaches land and ocean as rain, snow, sleet, or hail. Not all clouds produce precipitation.

⚖️
Different processes: Condensation forms liquid droplets; precipitation is water falling from clouds. Gravity acts before, during, and after these events. It does not take turns with the Sun.
📚 Instructional Design
Why this section exists
  • Explain how vapor becomes clouds and rain.
  • Check that students separate condensation from precipitation; not every cloud produces precipitation.
Cognitive science
  • Contrast (condensation vs evaporation)
  • Cause-and-effect (cooling to droplets)
  • Linking force to motion

Explore · Water on Land

Water on and Under the Ground

Water on land may flow across the surface, enter the ground, evaporate, or remain stored. Snow and ice can store water until melting lets it follow another pathway, such as runoff.

🌊
Flowing Across the Surface

When rain falls on a hillside, some water flows downhill across the surface into streams and rivers. This is runoff. Gravity helps this surface water flow toward lower places.

Key idea: Runoff

Runoff is water flowing across the land surface. It may enter streams and lakes, collect temporarily, or infiltrate along the way. Surface flow and infiltration are connected pathways, not permanently separate tracks.

🟩
Soaking Into the Ground

Not all of the water stays on top. Some of it soaks into the soil and rock below.

Key idea: Infiltration

Infiltration is water entering the ground. Some infiltrated water stays in soil and can be taken up by roots or evaporate. Some moves deeper; entering the ground does not instantly make all water groundwater.

Key idea: Groundwater

Groundwater occupies saturated pores and fractures in soil and rock: spaces filled with water. It can remain stored or move through those spaces, connecting with springs, streams, lakes, and oceans.

Many possible journeys: Rain could stay in soil, enter a plant, and return to the air through transpiration. Other water could remain underground or join a stream. Water does not have to return to the ocean before another pathway begins.
📚 Instructional Design
Why this section exists
  • Distinguish infiltration, soil water, and groundwater storage and flow.
  • Groundwater fills saturated pores and fractures, not giant underground caverns.
Cognitive science
  • Comparison (surface vs underground path)
  • Cause-and-effect (gravity to flow)
  • Connections among surface water, soil, plants, and groundwater

Explain · Wrap-Up

One System, Many Pathways

Return to the drying puddle. Its water can become invisible vapor while staying within Earth. Use the processes below to explain a possible journey, including a place it could stay stored.

Solar Energy Helps Water Change State
Energy from the Sun powers evaporation and transpiration.
Solar energy supplies much of the energy for liquid water to become invisible water vapor from exposed wet surfaces and inside leaves.
Clouds Form; Some Produce Precipitation
Cooling can cause condensation; gravity pulls falling precipitation downward.
Moist air cooling enough allows condensation into droplets. Clouds may contain droplets and/or ice. If particles grow large enough, they can fall as precipitation.
Water Moves or Stays Stored
Reservoirs connect through many possible pathways.
Water may flow downhill as runoff, enter the ground by infiltration, or stay stored in lakes, soil, groundwater, or snow and ice.
