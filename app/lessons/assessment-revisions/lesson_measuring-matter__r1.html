Measuring Matter

Everything around you - the air, your desk, even you - is made of matter. Scientists describe it with three measurements: mass, volume, and density.

Key Vocabulary

Words You'll Meet

Select a word to explore its meaning.

Matter

Anything that has mass and takes up space (volume).

The most common states of matter are solid, liquid, and gas.

Mass

The amount of matter in an object.

Mass is measured in grams (g) with a triple beam balance.

Volume

The amount of space an object takes up.

Volume is found in cubic centimeters (cm³) by multiplying length × width × height, or measured in milliliters (mL) with a graduated cylinder.

Density

A measure of how much matter is packed into a space.

Density is the ratio of mass to volume: D = m ÷ V, in g/cm³ or g/mL.

Triple Beam Balance

The lab tool scientists use to measure mass in grams.

You slide the riders along the three beams until the pointer lines up with zero.

Graduated Cylinder

A tall, marked container used to measure the volume of liquids (and odd-shaped objects) in milliliters (mL).

Buoyancy

The upward force a fluid exerts on an object placed in it.

This upward push is also called the buoyant force. An object floats when the buoyant force balances its weight.

Water Displacement

The movement of water out of the way when an object enters or moves through it.

The water pushed aside takes up the same volume as the part of the object that is underwater. It helps you measure volume, and it causes the buoyant force.

What Is Matter?

Look around you. Your desk, your water bottle, the air you're breathing - what do they all have in common?

Predict
🤔

Key idea: Matter

Check your prediction: air is matter, just like a rock.

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space (volume). That includes air! Air has mass (a filled balloon has slightly more mass than an empty one) and it takes up space (that's why the balloon gets bigger). Mass is not the same as weight. Weight is the pull of gravity on an object's mass. The most common states of matter are solid, liquid, and gas.

How Do We Measure Matter?

Scientists describe matter with three measurements. Follow the color coding through the whole lesson: teal is mass, green is volume, and orange is density.

Measurement 1
⚖️
Mass
Mass is the amount of matter in an object. It is measured using a triple beam balance or digital scale.
Tools: Triple Beam Balance or Digital ScaleUnit: grams (g)
Measurement 2
📦
Volume
Volume is the amount of space an object takes up. Use a ruler to calculate the volume of a regular-shaped object, or a graduated cylinder to measure the volume of a liquid or an irregularly shaped object using water displacement.
Tools: Ruler or Graduated CylinderUnits: cm³ or mL
Measurement 3
🪨
Density
Density describes how much mass is packed into a certain volume. It is the ratio of mass to volume and is calculated by dividing an object's mass by its volume.
Formula: Mass ÷ VolumeUnits: g/cm³ or g/mL

Explore · Packing It In

Density: The Ratio of Mass to Volume

Remember the bowling ball and the beach ball? Same size, totally different heft. Density is the measurement that explains why.

Key idea: Density

Density = Mass ÷ Volume

Density is the ratio of mass to volume: how much matter is packed into a space. With mass in grams and volume in cm³, density is in g/cm³. A volume in mL gives g/mL, the same number, because 1 mL = 1 cm³.

🎳 Bowling ball: lots of mass packed into its volume → high density.
🏖️ Beach ball: very little mass spread across about the same volume → low density.

Explain · Meet Buoyancy

Why Does Water Push Up?

Key idea: Buoyancy

When an object goes into water, it pushes water aside. This is called water displacement. The water pushes back up. That upward push is the buoyant force.

An object floats when the upward buoyant force balances its weight.

Every 1 mL of water pushed aside can hold up 1 g. Sinking objects get this push too, just not enough of it.

Boat Shape Investigation

Same Mass. Different Shape.

Step 1 · Predict
Two objects are made from the same material and each has a mass of 40 g. One is a compact solid. The other is shaped like a hollow boat. Which will float?

Step 2 · Investigate
Mass
Volume
Water pushed aside
↓ Weight
↑ Buoyant force

The material is still 2 g/cm³. The boat's average density is lower because its volume includes air.

About this model
  • Fresh water: each 1 mL pushed aside can hold up 1 g. 1 mL is the same space as 1 cm³.
  • Both shapes are 40 g of a 2 g/cm³ material, so the material takes up 20 cm³. Reshaping never changes the material's density.
  • The solid is a cube about 2.7 cm on each side. The boat is an open box 8 cm long, 5 cm wide, and 3 cm tall: 20 cm³ of material around 100 cm³ of air, 120 cm³ in all.
  • Average density of the whole object: solid 40 g ÷ 20 cm³ = 2 g/cm³; boat 40 g ÷ 120 cm³ ≈ 0.33 g/cm³, less than water's 1 g/mL.
  • A boat that fills with water acts like its 20 cm³ of material and sinks.

🧠 Show Your Thinking

Solve the Floating Giant Mystery

Write your own explanation first. Then submit your work to compare your thinking with a model answer.

How can a giant steel ship float while a tiny pebble sinks? Explain your thinking using the words mass, volume, and density. Consider how the ship's shape and the water it displaces help it float.

One strong way to say it Steel itself is denser than water, so a solid block of steel sinks. A ship's hull is different: it is hollow and wide, so it surrounds a lot of space, much of it filled with air. Divide the ship's total mass by its whole volume, including that space, and its average density is less than water's. As the hull settles into the water, it pushes aside, or displaces, water, and the water pushes back up on the hull. This upward push is called the buoyant force, or buoyancy. The ship settles until that upward push balances its weight, and then it floats. A pebble is solid rock, which is denser than water. Even fully underwater, it can push aside only its own small volume of water. That is the most upward push it can get, and it is still smaller than its weight, so it sinks. A strong answer uses mass, volume, and density, and explains how the ship's shape lets it displace enough water to float.

Extension
Density at Home

Find the density of a small object with an irregular shape, like a rock or a metal bolt.

  1. Measure the object's mass in grams on a balance or kitchen scale. Record it.
  2. Fill a graduated cylinder about halfway with water. Read the level at eye level, at the bottom of the curved water surface. Record this initial volume.
  3. Tilt the cylinder and gently slide the object in until it is completely underwater. Don't drop it in.
  4. Read and record the final water volume.
  5. Subtract: final volume minus initial volume. The difference is the object's volume in milliliters (mL).
  6. Divide mass by volume to find its density in g/mL.

Choose objects that fit inside the cylinder, sink on their own, and don't soak up water, dissolve, or react with water. Good choices: a marble, a small rock, a metal nut or bolt, or a few coins. Skip sponges, wood, sugar cubes, chalk, and anything electronic. If an object floats, you already know its density is less than 1 g/mL.

Then predict which of five household objects will float and which will sink. Test your predictions in a bowl of water. Do the results match what you'd expect from comparing each object's density to water's?

No graduated cylinder at home? Do this investigation in class.
