Vocabulary to Know

Words You'll Meet

Choose a card to see what each word means.

Resource

A material or energy source people use.

Renewable

Supplied by ongoing natural energy flows, or replenished by natural processes on human timescales.

Nonrenewable

A finite supply that natural processes do not replenish on human timescales, such as fossil fuels or uranium.

Per Capita

Per person. Here it is a citywide average: total daily water use divided by population.

Consumption

The amount of a resource used. The NYC data report water used, not water destroyed.

Efficiency

Providing the same service with less of a resource, such as less water per flush.

Conservation

Reducing resource use or waste through practices, such as fixing leaks.

Mitigation

Reducing an impact or its severity. It does not mean removing every impact.

Evidence

Observations, measurements, or documented findings used to support or challenge a claim.

📚 Instructional Design

Pre-teach the terms students need to read the evidence. Per capita and consumption carry the data reading; efficiency, conservation, and mitigation carry the argument. One card opens at a time.

Explore · Quick Review

Renewable or Nonrenewable?

⛏️

Nonrenewable

Finite supplies. Natural processes do not replenish them on human timescales.

Examples coal, petroleum, natural gas, uranium

🌱

Renewable

Ongoing energy flows, or supplies that natural processes replenish.

Examples sunlight, wind, flowing water, Earth's heat, biomass, freshwater

⚠️

Renewable does not mean unlimited local availability or no environmental impacts.

Using sunlight does not make tomorrow’s sunlight arrive faster or slower. But a supply such as freshwater or biomass can run short in one place if it is used faster than it is replenished.

📚 Instructional Design

Classification supports the investigation; it is not the Grade 7 argument performance. The one caveat distinguishes energy flows from replenishable supplies, and renewability from sustainable local use.

Explore · Energy Sources

Where the Energy Comes From

Nonrenewable fuels

Fossil fuels form over millions of years, but not all through the same pathway.

  1. Ancient land plants
  2. Burial and compaction
  3. Geologic change over millions of years
  4. Coal
  1. Remains of microscopic aquatic organisms
  2. Sediment burial
  3. Heating and geologic change over millions of years
  4. Petroleum and natural gas

Natural gas can also form in other ways.

⚛️ Nuclear: Uranium is a finite, mined resource, so nuclear energy is nonrenewable. Uranium is not a fossil fuel.

Renewable sources

Solar

Panels capture incoming energy from sunlight. Available sunlight changes with time of day and weather.

Explore · The Model

Population, Average Use, and Total Demand

Cities depend on several natural resources. We will examine a real water-use record to investigate how population, use per person, and technology interact.

👥Number of people
×
🚰Average water use per person each day
=
🏙️Total water use each day

people × gallons/person/day = gallons/day

Worked example: NYC, 2010

8,175,133 × 127.09 ≈ 1,039,000,000 gallons/day

≈ because the per-person figure is rounded.

  • Both numbers can change. More people increase total use if average use stays the same. Lower average use can offset population growth; the size of both changes matters.
  • It is an average. Per capita use covers all city uses, not only homes, so it is not identical individual behavior. Water used and environmental damage are also not identical quantities.
📚 Instructional Design

Use the units and a worked example without requiring algebra. The population denominator distributes aggregate city water consumption; it is not a measurement of every resident’s personal behavior.

B

Evidence B The water supply

Context

NYC’s freshwater comes through a supply system of reservoirs, lakes, and aqueducts. Total water demand measures use of that supply. Lower demand can reduce pressure on the supply; it does not itself measure water quality, ecological damage, or reservoir recovery.

Context: NYC DEP, Drinking Water.

Think

  1. What does total consumption tell you about demand on the supply? What does it not measure?
C

Evidence C A documented program

Reported finding
1994–1997 Toilet Rebate Program
≈ 1.3 million toilets replaced
≈ 90 million gallons/day in DEP-reported reduced consumption attributed to the program

Newer toilets use less water per flush for the same service. DEP’s figure is an approximate program savings estimate; this was not a controlled experiment.

Source: NYC DEP, Water Conservation Report - Annual Update, June 2011, page 15.

Think

  1. What can DEP’s finding establish, and what would go beyond it?
  2. Which values and findings would matter to a town considering water-efficient toilets?
Check your thinking

Supported: water-efficient toilets can help reduce freshwater demand.

Not established: The program did not necessarily cause the entire 1990–2010 decline. Other conservation and system-management changes also occurred, including metering and hydrant management. These findings do not establish improved water quality, ecosystem recovery, reservoir increases, or identical future savings in another town.

📚 Instructional Design

Evidence interpretation: preserve the frozen values rather than replacing them with a later dataset vintage. Per-capita figures are aggregate averages. DEP’s approximate program savings are an attribution from its report, not a controlled estimate of the entire trend. Questions sit beside each evidence piece; the two reveals check data reading (endpoint percentages calculated from the three rows) and causal limits after students answer. No model argument is supplied before submission.

Quick Recall

Quick Recall · 1 of 3

How is a per-person average different from a city total?

Show answer

The city total is all the water the city uses each day. The per-person average divides that total by the population.

Quick Recall · 2 of 3

How is a claim different from a measurement used as evidence?

Show answer

A claim is what you argue. A measurement is an observation that can support or challenge it.

Quick Recall · 3 of 3

Why does a mitigation argument need a link between a change and its effect?

Show answer

Two changes at the same time do not show that one caused the other. A mechanism explains how the change could reduce the impact.

One possible argument. Compare it with your own. Replacing older toilets could help a growing town reduce freshwater demand. NYC’s population increased from 7,335,650 in 1990 to 8,175,133 in 2010, while average daily use fell from 194.09 to 127.09 gallons/person/day and total demand fell from 1,423.8 to 1,039.0 million gallons/day. Lower average use more than offset the population increase in this case. DEP reported that its 1994–1997 program replaced approximately 1.3 million toilets and reduced consumption by approximately 90 million gallons/day. Using less water per flush can lower average use and therefore demand on freshwater supplies. Other changes also occurred, so the program does not explain the entire decline. These findings cannot predict the town’s exact savings without information about its fixtures, use, and future population. They also do not establish improved water quality.

Connections

The resources we power our lives with connect to energy itself, the carbon cycle, and the choices that shape the planet. These lessons show how.

🔋
Forms of Energy
Before a fuel, a sunbeam, or a gust of wind becomes electricity, it is energy in one form. Knowing those forms helps distinguish stored energy from incoming energy flows.
💥
Energy Transfer
A power plant or a solar panel is really a chain of energy moving from one place to another. See how that transfer turns a resource into usable power.
♻️
The Carbon Cycle
Coal, oil, and gas hold carbon that took millions of years to bury. See where that carbon goes once we burn nonrenewable fuels to release it.
🏭
Human Impacts on Ecosystems
Which resources we use, and how fast, is one of the biggest choices shaping the planet. See how those choices ripple out through living systems.
