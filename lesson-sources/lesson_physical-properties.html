Physical Properties of Matter

Key Vocabulary

Words You'll Meet

A physical property is something you can observe or measure without changing what a material is.

Force & Shape

Flexibility

Bends without breaking.

Example: Garden hose

Malleability

Can be flattened without breaking.

Example: Aluminum foil

Ductility

Can be drawn into a wire.

Example: Copper wire

Hardness

Resists scratching.

Example: Diamond drill tip

Remember: Flexibility = bend · Ductility = wire · Malleability = flatten

Heat

Thermal Conductivity

How easily heat moves through a material.

Example: Metal pan

Melting Point

The temperature at which a solid becomes a liquid.

Example: Ice melts at 0°C

Electricity & Magnetism

Electrical Conductivity

How easily electric current flows through a material.

Example: Copper wire

Magnetism

Whether a material is attracted to a magnet.

Example: Iron or steel

📚 Instructional Design
Why these investigations exist
  • Students test seven properties from the family cards (hardness, electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity, magnetism, melting point, malleability, ductility) before using some of the same tests on the Mystery Cube.
  • Each uses its own visual mechanism: a scratch mark, a glowing bulb, a heat front with a thermometer, a magnet pull, a melting sample, a press, and a wire-drawing die.
  • No prediction is required before testing. Each Discovery line appears only after the student has gathered the evidence for it.
Hardness model
  • Representative samples: paraffin wax, hard acrylic plastic, soft (nearly pure) aluminum, ordinary window glass; tools: fingernail, pure copper coin, ordinary steel nail.
  • Ordered comparison, not hardness numbers: wax < fingernail < copper coin, acrylic, and soft aluminum (close in hardness) < steel nail and glass (close in hardness). A tool scratches only a clearly softer sample; close pairs give no clear scratch.
  • Real alloys and plastics vary; a hard aluminum alloy or a different plastic can change the result.
Circuit and heat models
  • Circuit: a low-voltage classroom battery and bulb. Copper and aluminum conduct; plastic, rubber, and dry wood act as insulators here. Moisture or very high voltage can change that.
  • Heat: identical rods, the same heat, and an ordering taken from real thermal conductivities (copper far above steel, steel far above glass and wood). Times are illustrative, not measured.
Magnet, melting, press, and wire models
  • Magnet: iron and ordinary (carbon) steel are attracted; aluminum, copper, and plastic are not. Some stainless steels (austenitic grades) are only weakly attracted, so the model names ordinary steel.
  • Melting: ice starts at -10°C, coconut oil at 10°C, wax, bismuth, and gold at room temperature (20°C). Students choose +5°C or +100°C steps (metals default to +100°C). Pure ice melts at 0°C, pure bismuth at 271.4°C, pure gold at 1,064.2°C. Coconut oil (a fat mixture, about 23 to 26°C) and paraffin wax (a mixture; grades range roughly 46 to 68°C) are shown at about 25°C and 55°C.
  • Melting simplification: the step that reaches or passes the melting point is reported as melting at the melting point, even when the displayed temperature has gone past it; the next step finishes the change. In reality, a pure substance stays at its melting point while it melts, and mixtures melt across a range.
  • Press: aluminum and copper flatten (malleable); glass and chalk crack (brittle). Ordinary room-temperature samples under slow squeezing; not a strength test. Malleability is flattening under squeezing force, not bending (flexibility) or drawing into wire (ductility). The sample responds only when the press reaches it.
  • Wire drawing: a pointed rod is pulled through a narrowing die. Copper, aluminum, and gold draw into wire (gold is the most ductile metal); glass and chalk snap at room temperature. Glass softened by heat can be drawn into fibers, which the caption notes.
Accessibility considerations
  • Every choice is a labeled button with aria-pressed; results are announced in text, not only by color or glow.
  • Motion is short and switches off with reduced-motion settings.

Cargo Ship Challenge

Water pushes up on anything placed in it. That upward push is called buoyancy. A steel ship can float because its hollow shape allows it to displace enough water to support its weight.

Why does it float?

A ship floats because of buoyancy, the upward force of water.

  • Displacement: A ship pushes water out of the way as it sinks lower.
  • Balance: A floating ship displaces water that weighs the same as the ship.
  • Sinking: If the ship becomes too heavy to displace enough water, it sinks.

The ship's wide, hollow hull helps it displace enough water to float.

📚 Instructional Design
Why this section exists
  • Students discover that a floating ship pushes aside its own mass in water, and that more cargo means a deeper ship and more displaced water.
  • Shows a coherent limit: past 600 g this hull cannot push aside enough water, so it floods and sinks.
  • The hollow-hull line and the intro's displacement sentence answer the hook's ship question; density itself is taught in Measuring Matter.
Cognitive science
  • The ship is visible and usable at once; the optional prediction is checked after the first cargo change and never gates the experiment.
  • Immediate cause and effect: the hull drops a fixed step per crate while the waterline stays still.
  • The challenge asks students to find the limit by experimenting, not by reading.
Bloom's / DOK
  • Apply to Analyze
  • DOK 2 (relate mass to displaced water and predict a floating limit)
Accessibility considerations
  • Large add, remove, and reset buttons; no dragging.
  • Every measurement and the ship's status are written as text and announced politely.
  • The drawing's description updates with each change.

About these tests

🧠 Show Your Thinking

Scientists don't just know the answer. They explain their thinking.

Write your own explanation first. Then submit your work to compare your thinking with a model answer.

In one sentence, explain what a physical property is, and how a scientist could use physical properties to identify an unknown material without changing what it is.

One strong way to say it A physical property is a characteristic you can observe or measure - like density, magnetism, hardness, melting point, or conductivity - without changing what the material is, so a scientist can identify an unknown material by measuring its combination of properties and matching that fingerprint to a known substance. If your sentence says physical properties can be measured without changing the material and used to identify it, you have it.

🔍 The Mystery You Came In With You started this lesson with one question: "Why does a giant steel ship float while a tiny steel coin sinks?" If you can explain it with density, volume, and buoyancy, you've solved the mystery.
